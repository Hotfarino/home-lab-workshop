01 · MEET THE DRIVE TEAM

One team. Several physical drives.

RAID means Redundant Array of Independent Disks. An array is a group of drives that work together. Redundant means keeping extra information so a supported failure does not have to stop your work. RAID 0 is the exception: it keeps no redundant information.

Imagine a class making a giant poster. One plan splits the work. Another makes two matching posters. A third saves clues that can rebuild a missing part. Those are the three ideas behind striping, mirroring, and parity.

1 · App“Save this file.”
2 · WindowsFinds the volume and file blocks.
3 · RAID layerMaps blocks to the member drives.
4 · DrivesStore the data and any redundancy.

RAID helps with availability. A backup helps you recover. A mirror also copies an unwanted change. Parity also updates after a deletion. Keep separate, recoverable backups.

The words you need before starting
Physical drive vs. logical volume
A physical drive is a real storage device. A logical volume is storage the operating system can use. A hardware RAID controller may show several physical drives as one virtual disk. That disk can contain partitions and volumes.
Block vs. stripe
A block is a small unit of data. A stripe spreads a group of blocks across array members. A block marked A here is a teaching label, not a whole file or a real block size.
Read vs. write
A read gets stored data. A write saves or changes data. The diagram shows both directions.
HDD vs. SSD
HDD means hard disk drive: spinning magnetic storage. SSD means solid-state drive: storage with no moving platters. Compatible RAID systems can use either. RAID is the layout, not the type of drive.

RAID 0: the fast relay team

What it does: Break data into pieces and spread them across members. With two drives, a simple layout is A/C/E on drive 1 and B/D/F on drive 2.

Write: The RAID layer sends each new block to its assigned member. Read: It gets the blocks from their locations and returns the logical sequence.

Why a failure hurts: If drive 2 disappears, B, D and F have no alternate source. A, C and E alone cannot restore the complete array. Losing the array does not mean every surviving disk is physically blank.

Example: Four 2 TB drives give 8 TB before overhead. Use cases include temporary rendering or scratch work that can be recreated.

Memory hook: RAID 0 = zero redundancy. More drives do not make it fault tolerant.

RAID 1: the matching notebooks

What it does: Store the same logical data on both members of a mirror. Every A has another A. Extra copies cost capacity.

Write: Update both copies. Read: The implementation can read from either member and may spread independent read requests between them.

After a failure: Use the remaining copy. A replacement needs the surviving data copied onto it. An empty replacement is not instantly a complete mirror.

Example: Two 2 TB drives give 2 TB before overhead. A small server’s operating-system volume is a common use case.

Memory hook: Two notebooks, one set of information. A mirror is not a version history.

RAID 5: one recovery clue per row

What it does: Stripe data and calculated parity across at least three drives. The drive holding parity changes between stripes.

Write: Save the new data and matching parity. A small change may need old data and old parity to be read first. A complete stripe can be handled differently.

Read: With healthy members, read the requested data blocks. After one member fails, combine surviving blocks and parity when missing data is needed.

Example: Four 2 TB drives give 6 TB before overhead. The parity overhead totals 2 TB, but it is spread across drives. There is no single permanent “parity drive” in this layout.

Memory hook: RAID 5 = one missing drive is recoverable under normal assumptions. A second failure before recovery exceeds that protection.

RAID 6: two different recovery clues

What it does: Stripe data with two independent parity values. Our drawing calls them P and Q.

Why two clues matter: One equation cannot generally find two unknown blocks. Two independent relationships can provide the missing information. Q is not just another copy of P.

Write and read: Writes maintain both parity relationships. Reads can use existing data or reconstruct missing blocks within the two-drive limit.

Example: Four 2 TB drives give 4 TB; six 2 TB drives give 8 TB before overhead. Any two members may fail. A third failure exceeds the level’s protection.

Memory hook: RAID 6 = two parity values, two-drive tolerance. It belongs in the current 220-1201 objectives.

RAID 10: buddy teams on a relay

What it does: Make mirror pairs, then stripe across the pairs. In this guide, drives 1/2 are pair 1 and drives 3/4 are pair 2.

Write: Put A on both members of pair 1; put B on both members of pair 2. Read: Fetch each needed block from a working member of its pair.

Watch the pattern: Lose 1 and 3: the other buddy in each pair survives. Lose 1 and 2: the entire first pair is gone. The total number of failures alone does not answer the question.

Example: Four 2 TB drives give 4 TB before overhead. With six paired drives, up to three failures can be survived only if each occurs in a different pair.

Memory hook: RAID 10 always tolerates any one member failure in this layout. “Survives any two” is false.

The number is a layout, not a score

RAID 10 does not mean ten drives. RAID 6 is not simply “six times faster.” Choose a level by required capacity, tolerated failures, workload, hardware support and cost.

RAID 10 vs. RAID 0+1: RAID 10 stripes across mirrors. RAID 0+1 mirrors stripe sets. They are different arrangements; do not swap the names on a diagram.

JBOD: “Just a Bunch Of Disks” may mean separate drives or concatenated storage, depending on the product. It does not automatically mean RAID or redundancy.

Faster? Parallel work can improve throughput. Real results depend on drive speed, controller, interface, cache, workload and failure state. There is no universal “RAID doubles speed” rule.

The drawings use example placement. Actual block order, chunk size and parity rotation can vary while following the same level’s protection rules.

Build a capacity estimate.

Choose the active members, then change their sizes. Watch space divide into useful data, redundancy and unused extra capacity.

Walk through the arithmetic
  1. Count the active members. Leave a hot spare out of this count.
  2. Find the smallest member. This model uses that much space from every active drive.
  3. Use the level’s formula. Four 2 TB members in RAID 5: (4 − 1) × 2 = 6 TB usable.
  4. Account for extras separately. A fifth 2 TB drive assigned as a hot spare makes installed capacity 10 TB, but usable array capacity remains 6 TB.
  5. Expect the formatted result to be lower after metadata and file-system overhead. A manufacturer’s TB is decimal; 1 TB = 1,000,000,000,000 bytes. 1 TiB = 1,099,511,627,776 bytes. Unit display also affects the number you see.

This is a conventional fixed-width estimate. It does not model vendor expansion schemes, thin provisioning, compression, deduplication or Storage Spaces slab placement.

Two pieces make one clue

This models one RAID 5 stripe with two data blocks and one parity block. Other stripes put parity on a different member. RAID 6 needs an additional independent coding relationship; duplicating this P does not create Q.

Why changing one block also changes parity

Start with A = 1010 and B = 1100. P = 0110. If A changes to 0010 while B stays the same, new P becomes 1110. Leaving old P behind would break the relationship used for recovery.

A common small-write approach reads old data and parity, calculates updated parity, then writes new data and parity. Full-stripe writes, caches and controllers can use different strategies. This is why parity overhead is about work, not just capacity.

06 · WHEN A DRIVE SAYS “CRASH!”

A surviving array still needs attention.

HealthyMembers and redundancy are ready.
DegradedA member is missing; enough information remains.
RebuildingRestore missing member data to a replacement.
Verified healthyCheck status, logs, files and backups.

An array can keep serving files while degraded. That does not mean the bad drive healed. Rebuilding reads surviving storage and writes the replacement, so it can slow normal work. More failures or unreadable sectors can interrupt recovery.

Hot spare ≠ hot swap

Hot spare: A compatible standby drive assigned for recovery. It normally adds no usable data capacity while waiting. When eligible, it can become the rebuild target.

Hot swap: Hardware and software support for replacing a drive while the system runs. A spare is a role; hot swap is a replacement capability.

Cold spare: A replacement kept offline until someone installs it.

A spare shortens the wait for a replacement. It does not make RAID 5 survive two simultaneous member failures before the missing information is restored.

Rebuild ≠ restore

Rebuild: Re-create a missing member from surviving redundancy. It needs enough readable information in the array.

Restore: Recover data from a separate backup. It can recover a prior version if that version exists in the backup.

Resync: Bring copies or parity relationships back into agreement. The exact operation depends on the storage system.

RAID 0 has no redundant member information to rebuild a missing drive. A blank replacement cannot guess its lost blocks.

Follow a sensible repair sequence

  1. Read the alert. Record the array, controller, failed member, slot and serial number. An LED color or beep pattern depends on the manufacturer.
  2. Check the recovery position. Is the array still accessible? How many members are missing? Are backups current and usable? Follow the organization’s recovery plan.
  3. Confirm the exact drive. Compare the controller’s report with the bay label or supported locate function. Pulling a working member can turn a degraded array into an unavailable one.
  4. Follow the service procedure. Confirm drive compatibility and sufficient usable capacity. Verify hot-swap support; otherwise use the required shutdown procedure. Use appropriate electrostatic-discharge precautions.
  5. Rebuild under the controller’s instructions. A configured spare may start automatically; other systems need an explicit action. Do not initialize or recreate an existing array as a routine fix.
  6. Monitor to completion. Check progress, remaining-drive health and error logs. A new disk being detected does not mean recovery has finished.
  7. Verify and document. Confirm healthy status, access to expected data and functioning backups. Record what failed and what changed.

Spot the symptoms without jumping to a diagnosis

Clicking or grinding: Possible mechanical HDD trouble. An SSD has no spinning mechanism to make that kind of drive noise.

Missing drive or array: Check power, cabling, controller state and drivers as appropriate. A missing array is not automatically a failed physical disk.

Slow I/O: A rebuild, degraded reads, workload, cache policy, interface limits or a failing device can all contribute.

S.M.A.R.T. warning: Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology reports drive health indicators. It does not predict every failure. Controller visibility and tool support vary.

Corrupt files: Redundancy alone is not proof that every stored byte is correct. Investigate the cause and use appropriate recovery tools or backups.

07 · WHERE THE RAID LAYER LIVES

Windows sees storage through several views.

Hardware RAID

A dedicated controller manages member drives and exposes virtual disks to the operating system. It may have its own processor and protected cache. Use its management utility to inspect member health, parity state, spare assignments and rebuild progress.

The controller can be on a PCIe card or integrated into the system. PCIe means Peripheral Component Interconnect Express; it describes the expansion connection, not a RAID level.

Software and firmware-assisted RAID

Software RAID uses an operating-system storage layer and host resources. Firmware-assisted arrangements depend on platform firmware plus drivers; a motherboard RAID menu does not by itself prove there is a full dedicated hardware controller.

HBA means host bus adapter. It connects the host to storage. An HBA in passthrough mode is not necessarily making RAID arrays. Check the exact device and mode.

From blank lab drives to a usable volume
  1. Plan: Choose capacity, tolerated failures and a supported RAID level.
  2. Prepare: Use disposable lab drives or preserve needed data first. Creating an array may destroy existing contents.
  3. Configure: In the owning controller or software tool, select the exact members, level, virtual-disk size and any spare. Use supported settings for stripe size and cache policy.
  4. Initialize: Follow the tool’s initialization process. It prepares the array and any redundancy; RAID metadata records how the members fit together. Some systems do background work after the virtual disk appears.
  5. Present: Ensure Windows has the correct storage driver and sees the intended virtual disk.
  6. Organize: Create the appropriate partition/volume and file system, then assign a drive letter or mount point. RAID layout, partitioning and formatting are separate tasks.
  7. Verify: Test reads and writes, check healthy status and set up monitoring plus tested backups. A successful format alone does not verify drive-failure recovery.

Paths can vary by Windows version, edition and hardware. These are labeled teaching views, not screenshots or readings from your PC.

Storage Spaces: a related idea, with its own rules

Windows Storage Spaces groups eligible drives into a pool and creates virtual storage spaces with a chosen resiliency. Simple, mirror and parity choices use similar ideas, but do not assume every option is an exact classic RAID layout.

In Windows 11, look under Settings → System → Storage → Advanced storage settings → Storage Spaces, or search for “Storage Spaces.” Available options and drive-count requirements vary by implementation. Use Microsoft’s documentation for the exact resiliency.

Disk Management, Storage Spaces and a hardware controller utility do different jobs. Use the tool that owns the storage configuration. Creating a pool, initializing a disk or formatting can erase data; practice configuration only on disposable lab storage.

Microsoft: Storage Spaces in Windows

Why the cache and power supply matter

Cache is a fast holding area. In write-through mode, the controller waits for a drive write before reporting completion. In write-back mode, it may report completion after receiving data in cache, then write that data to drives later.

Pending cached writes need the appropriate power-loss protection. Battery- or flash-backed cache and drive-level protection depend on the system. A UPS, or uninterruptible power supply, helps with power interruptions but is not a substitute for every form of cache protection.

A controller may switch policies when its protection is unhealthy. That can change performance without changing the RAID level. Never assume a fast acknowledgment means data already reached every physical drive.

08 · SPEED HAS MORE THAN ONE MEANING

Match the work to the design.

Throughput: how much per second?

A long video copy moves lots of data in order. Throughput measures transferred data over time, often in MB/s. Multiple drives may share that work, but the controller, connection or network can become the bottleneck.

A SATA label or PCIe link speed is not a guaranteed file-transfer speed. RAID cannot make a slower shared connection carry unlimited data.

IOPS and latency: small requests

IOPS means input/output operations per second: how many reads or writes complete each second. Latency is how long a request takes.

A busy database may send many small random requests. That is different from copying one large file. Mirrors and parity layouts can behave differently under these workloads.

Exam habit: Read every requirement. “Fastest,” “most capacity,” “any two failures,” and “lowest drive count” ask different questions. Check failure tolerance before doing capacity math.

A practical planning checklist
  1. How much usable space is required?
  2. How many and which drive failures must be tolerated?
  3. Is the workload mostly reads, large sequential writes, or small random writes?
  4. Which layouts and drive types does the actual controller or software support?
  5. How will alerts, spare drives, rebuilds and tested backups be managed?

RAID is local storage organization. It does not automatically add a second server, a second controller, a second power supply, encryption or an off-site copy.

11 · WORDS WITHOUT THE MYSTERY

Acronyms & quick definitions

RAID · Redundant Array of Independent Disks
A method of organizing multiple physical drives. Older material may use “Inexpensive” for the I; “Independent” matches the current A+ wording.
Redundancy · extra recovery information
Copies or calculated information that can help tolerate specified failures. More redundancy uses storage space.
Fault tolerance · continuing through a supported failure
The ability to keep working despite a failure within the design’s limits. It is not immunity to all loss.
Member · a drive participating in the array
A waiting dedicated spare is not an active member holding the array’s normal data stripes.
Stripe, mirror, parity
Stripe: spread blocks. Mirror: store matching copies. Parity: calculate recovery information from data.
Degraded, failed, rebuilding
Degraded: enough information remains, but some protection is missing. Failed/unavailable: the array cannot supply all required data. Rebuilding: reconstruct missing member information onto a target.
SATA · Serial Advanced Technology Attachment
A storage connection standard used by many HDDs and SSDs. It does not specify a RAID level.
SCSI · Small Computer System Interface; SAS · Serial Attached SCSI
SCSI is a family of storage command/interface standards. SAS carries SCSI over serial links and is common in enterprise storage. Compatibility depends on the controller, connectors and drives.
NVMe · Non-Volatile Memory Express
A protocol designed for nonvolatile storage, commonly over PCIe. M.2 is a form factor; an M.2 drive is not automatically NVMe. NVMe RAID requires appropriate support.
OS · operating system; MMC · Microsoft Management Console
Windows is an OS. MMC hosts administrative tools called snap-ins. Computer Management is one place to find Disk Management.
MB/s vs. Mb/s
Uppercase B means bytes. Lowercase b means bits. Eight bits make one byte. Do not compare transfer numbers without checking units.
TB vs. TiB
TB is decimal storage capacity. TiB is binary capacity. They measure the same bytes with different-sized units.
ESD · electrostatic discharge
A sudden transfer of static electricity that can damage electronics. Use the service manual’s handling and grounding precautions.

Three final checks

Answers and encouraging explanations
  1. 12 TB. Four 4 TB members in RAID 5: (4 − 1) × 4 = 12 TB before overhead. You set aside one member’s worth of capacity, not one dedicated parity disk.
  2. Drives 1 and 3 can fail together. With pairs 1/2 and 3/4, one buddy remains in each pair. Losing 1 and 2 removes the entire first pair.
  3. Restore a suitable backup version. RAID redundancy does not undo an accidental deletion. You now know the difference between keeping a service online and recovering old data.
